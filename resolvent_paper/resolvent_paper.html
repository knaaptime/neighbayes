Resolvent-Traces as Log-Determinant Gradients: One Object, Three Consumers for Scalable Bayesian Spatial Inference

Author

knaaptime

Published

July 26, 2026

Abstract

Gradient-based Bayesian samplers (NUTS/HMC, MALA, nutpie) need the \(\rho\)-derivative of the spatial Jacobian \(g(\rho) = \frac{d}{d\rho}\log|I - \rho W| = -\operatorname{tr}(W(I-\rho W)^{-1})\), the resolvent trace (Jacobi’s formula). We make three observations. First, the exact analytic derivative of each log-determinant surrogate in the companion log-determinant paper — Chebyshev–Cholesky, LU–AAA, stochastic Lanczos quadrature (SLQ) — is available in closed form, in microseconds, with no per-\(\rho\) solves; it agrees with jax.grad of the surrogate to \(10^{-13}\). The clean way to use it inside HMC is to freeze the Hutchinson probes, so the surrogate log-density is a smooth deterministic function and Hamiltonian conservation is preserved — no \(\rho\)-truncation anywhere, at the cost of an \(O(1/\sqrt{p})\) surrogate bias. Second, the same resolvent trace is the quantity spatial impacts need: the average direct multiplier \(\overline{\operatorname{diag}}((I-\rho W)^{-1}) = 1 - (\rho/n)g(\rho)\) and the SDM cross-direct \(\overline{\operatorname{diag}}((I-\rho W)^{-1}W) = -g(\rho)/n\) were previously built from an \(O(n^3)\) eigendecomposition; routing them through the model’s fast logdet gradient eliminates it entirely. Third, for the unrestricted three-parameter LeSage–Pace (2008) origin–destination flow model, each gradient component is a single matvec-only resolvent trace whose Hutchinson relative error scales as \(1/\sqrt{NP}\) and improves with flow size \(N = n^2\) — the first scalable, eigenvalue-free, unrestricted estimation path. The honest headline from our CPU benchmarks: blocked Gibbs still wins the cross-section sampler shootout (2.3–5.2\(\times\) more \(\rho\)-ESS/sec than native NUTS), but NUTS wins the random-effects panel shootout (1.3–1.4\(\times\) more \(\rho\)-ESS/sec than Gibbs) — the regime where the higher-dimensional posterior favours gradient mixing. Two durable wins survive — the \(\mu\)s surrogate derivative is the right NUTS ingredient (2\(\times\) faster inside the leapfrog than the eigenvalue logdet at identical mixing), and the gradient path caught a real Gibbs bug (a \(T\)-double-count in the panel logdet Jacobian that silently biased \(\rho\) toward zero, now fixed so both samplers agree at \(\rho \approx 0.49\)). GPU is the open door. All methods are implemented in the open-source neighbayes library.

Keywords

spatial econometrics, resolvent trace, log-determinant gradient, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, stochastic Lanczos quadrature, spatial impacts, origin-destination flow model, Kronecker gradient

1 Introduction

The spatial autoregressive (SAR) model (Ord 1975) specifies

\[ y = \rho W y + X\beta + \varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon \sim N(0,\sigma^2 I), \]

with reduced form \(y = (I-\rho W)^{-1}(X\beta + \varepsilon)\). The Jacobian \(\log|I_n - \rho W|\) enters the log-likelihood at every value of \(\rho\) the sampler visits, and the companion log-determinant paper shows how to evaluate it exactly and cheaply via sparse Cholesky–Chebyshev or LU–AAA interpolation. The same Jacobian appears in spatial error (SEM), spatial Durbin (SDM), and spatial panel models, and in the count/binary spatial generalised linear models that extend them.

Modern Bayesian inference increasingly wants gradient-based samplers: NUTS/HMC (Hoffman and Gelman 2014; Betancourt 2017), MALA, and the probabilistic-programming engines that route them — PyMC (Pati et al. 2024) via nutpie, or blackjax (Lao et al. 2020) directly. These samplers need not just the log-density value but its \(\rho\)-derivative. By Jacobi’s formula that derivative is the resolvent trace

\[ g(\rho) \;=\; \frac{d}{d\rho}\log|I_n - \rho W| \;=\; -\operatorname{tr}\!\big(W(I-\rho W)^{-1}\big) \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_i}{1-\rho\lambda_i}, \]

a single spectral sum that is, as we show, available in closed form from the same precompute that produces the value surrogate. This paper is about that single object and the three places the library needs it.

Note

What this paper is not. It is the gradient companion to the log-determinant paper, not a re-derivation of the value methods. We assume the chol-cheb / AAA / SLQ surrogates and their precomputes as given, and ask: what does a trustworthy, cheap \(g(\rho)\) buy, and what does it cost?

2 The frozen-probe surrogate and its exact derivative

2.1 Why fresh probes break HMC

The original pitch for a stochastic logdet gradient was: estimate \(g(\rho) = -\operatorname{tr}(W(I-\rho W)^{-1})\) unbiasedly with Hutchinson probes (Hutchinson 1990) and sparse solves, plug it into NUTS, and the sampler targets the exact posterior. Two facts make that pitch incoherent as stated.

(a) NUTS needs the value too. NUTS evaluates the log-density throughout tree building and in the Metropolis correction, so a value is required, not just a gradient. The unbiased gradient estimator gives no unbiased value estimator for free.

(b) Fresh probes break the Hamiltonian. Re-drawing the Hutchinson probes every leapfrog step makes the gradient a different function at each step. The leapfrog integrator assumes a single smooth Hamiltonian; with a re-randomised gradient the Hamiltonian is no longer conserved and detailed balance is lost. The Markov chain no longer targets a well-defined distribution.

The coherent statement is: freeze the probes. With fixed probes, the surrogate log-density

\[ \widehat{L}(\rho) \;=\; \text{(any logdet surrogate evaluated with frozen probes)} \]

is a smooth, deterministic function of \(\rho\), and its gradient \(\widehat{L}'(\rho)\) is the exact analytic derivative of that function. NUTS then samples a surrogate posterior whose log-density differs from the truth by \(O(1/\sqrt{p})\) (the frozen-probe SLQ bias), with no \(\rho\)-truncation anywhere. The per-\(\rho\) solves that the unbiased estimator needs are relegated to a reference / pseudo-marginal fallback role (see Section 11).

2.2 The surrogate derivative is already in the package

Every logdet method neighbayes ships evaluates a smooth surrogate \(\widehat{L}(\rho)\): Chebyshev–Cholesky, AAA, SLQ, stochastic Chebyshev, and the eigenvalue method. Its derivative \(\widehat{L}'(\rho)\) is the resolvent trace estimate, available in closed form in microseconds, with no per-step solves. neighbayes/_logdet/_resolvent.py implements them:

  • logdet_grad_chebyshev — the derivative of the Chebyshev-in-\(\rho\) surrogate \(\sum_j c_j T_j(x(\rho))\), computed by a differentiated Clenshaw recurrence (clenshaw_deriv_x) that carries \(b_k\) and \(\beta_k = db_k/dx\) through the same recurrence used to evaluate the value. This serves the chebyshev, cheb_cholesky, and cheb_stochastic methods, which all share the coefficient representation. W type: symmetric (rook lattice, polygon contiguity); the precompute uses sparse Cholesky on the D-symmetrised operator.
  • logdet_grad_aaa — the derivative of the AAA barycentric rational \(L(\rho) = N(\rho)/D(\rho)\), with \(N' = -\sum_j w_j f_j/(\rho-z_j)^2\) and \(D' = -\sum_j w_j/(\rho-z_j)^2\), exactly the derivative of the value form. W type: asymmetric (directed KNN, travel time, migration flows); the precompute uses sparse LU/KLU, and AAA’s poles capture the logarithmic singularities that polynomials must approximate by truncation order.
  • logdet_grad_slq — the derivative of the frozen-probe SLQ surrogate \(\frac{1}{P}\sum_i w_i \log(1-\rho\theta_i)\), i.e. \(g(\rho) = -\frac{1}{P}\operatorname{Re}\sum_i \frac{w_i\theta_i}{1-\rho\theta_i}\). W type: both — Lanczos for symmetric, Arnoldi for asymmetric; factorisation-free, so it survives \(n \gg 10^5\) where both Cholesky and LU fill-in become prohibitive.
  • logdet_grad_eigenvalue — the exact \(-\sum_i \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i/(1-\rho\lambda_i))\). W type: both (complex eigenvalues for asymmetric \(W\)), but \(O(n^3)\) setup limits it to \(n \lesssim 500\).

Because each formula is the analytic derivative of the corresponding value surrogate, it agrees with jax.grad / pytensor.grad of that surrogate to machine precision. The numpy path in make_logdet_grad_numpy_vec_fn and the autodiff paths therefore compute the same object, from the same coefficients, with no per-\(\rho\) solves. There is no separate make_logdet_grad_jax_fn: the JAX gradient is jax.grad(make_logdet_jax_fn(...)) — autodiff of the same value closure whose coefficients the numpy path differentiates by hand. The xp parametrisation of _resolvent exists so the formulas could be called with jax.numpy directly, but in practice the JAX path goes through jax.grad, not through _resolvent with JAX arrays.

2.3 The autodiff-parity gate

The internal correctness gate confirms that the numpy _resolvent core and jax.grad of the JAX closures compute the same object. Across chol-cheb (symmetric \(W\)), AAA (directed \(W\)), and SLQ, evaluated at \(\rho \in \{0.2, 0.5, 0.8\}\), the worst absolute difference is reported live from the benchmark CSV (the parity_gate row in Table 1). Both paths are also benchmarked head-to-head in the main accuracy table — chol_cheb vs jax_grad_chol_cheb (symmetric) and aaa vs jax_grad_aaa (asymmetric) — showing identical accuracy but very different per-eval cost (see Section 4). This is the foundation for the rest of the paper: a single closed-form gradient backs every consumer, accessed via two paths — explicit numpy for impacts/MALA/nutpie-custom, autodiff JAX for blackjax/numpyro. every consumer.

3 One object, three consumers

\(g(\rho) = -\operatorname{tr}(W(I-\rho W)^{-1})\) is a single quantity the library needs in three places:

Consumer Needs Today (pre-resolvent) With _resolvent
Gradient samplers (NUTS/MALA), any backend \(g(\rho)\) autodiff of pytensor/jax closures (implicit) explicit closed form + numpy factory
Spatial impacts (_chunked_eig_means) \(\overline{\lambda/(1-\rho\lambda)} = -g(\rho)/n\) dense eigenvalues, \(O(n^3)\), forms \(V, V^{-1}\) same quantity at any \(n\), no eigendecomposition
The joint log-density \(g(\rho)\) as its only extra \(\rho\)-term over OLS — —

The unification is why the core is backend-parametrised (xp = numpy or jax.numpy) rather than a blackjax-specific closure: the numpy path feeds nutpie-custom / MALA / impacts; the pytensor path already carries it for PyMC and nutpie-via-PyMC; the jax path for blackjax/numpyro. Same formulas, one implementation.

4 Experiment A: gradient accuracy and cost

Question. How accurately and how cheaply can each method deliver \(g(\rho)\)? We test two \(W\) families that route to different decomposition backends:

  • Symmetric \(W\) (rook contiguity on a 2D lattice, row-standardised): the degree-symmetrisation \(W_{\text{sym}} = D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}\) renders \(I - \rho W_{\text{sym}}\) SPD, so the precompute uses sparse Cholesky factorisation at Chebyshev nodes and the surrogate is a Chebyshev polynomial in \(\rho\) (chol-cheb).
  • Asymmetric \(W\) (directed KNN graph, row-standardised): no SPD symmetrisation is available, so the precompute uses sparse LU/KLU factorisation at an adaptive grid and the surrogate is an AAA barycentric rational in \(\rho\) — whose poles capture the logarithmic singularities that Chebyshev polynomials must approximate by truncation order.

Both feed the same _resolvent gradient core: logdet_grad_chebyshev (differentiated Clenshaw) for the former, logdet_grad_aaa (differentiated barycentric) for the latter. We also compare SLQ (factorisation-free, Lanczos/Arnoldi matvecs), an unbiased but noisy CG-resolvent estimator (fresh Hutchinson probes + sparse solves: CG for symmetric \(W\), BiCGSTAB for asymmetric \(W\)), and a Perron-deflated variant for the \(\rho \to 1\) regime. Ground truth is dense eigenvalues at small \(n\) and a central finite-difference of the exact sparse logdet at large \(n\).

The full script is in gradient_study.py; the autodiff-parity gate of Section 2.3 is run first.

Code 
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from pathlib import Path

RESULTS = Path("results")
df = pd.read_csv(RESULTS / "gradient_accuracy.csv")
# Aggregate to (family, n, method, param) -> max relerr over 0.3-0.9, relerr@0.99, mean eval µs
def agg(g):
    lo = g[(g["rho"] >= 0.3) & (g["rho"] <= 0.9)]
    hi = g[g["rho"] == 0.99]
    return pd.Series({
        "relerr 0.3–0.9 (max)": lo["relerr"].max(),
        "relerr @0.99": hi["relerr"].iloc[0] if len(hi) else np.nan,
        "eval µs": g["eval_s"].mean() * 1e6,
    })
summary = df.groupby(["family", "n", "method", "param"]).apply(agg, include_groups=False).reset_index()
# Add W type and decomposition columns
w_type = summary["family"].map({"rook": "symmetric", "knn": "asymmetric"}).fillna("?")
decomp = summary["family"].map({"rook": "sparse Cholesky", "knn": "sparse LU/KLU"}).fillna("?")
surr = summary["family"].map({"rook": "chol-cheb", "knn": "AAA"}).fillna("?")
summary.insert(0, "W type", w_type)
summary.insert(1, "decomposition", decomp)
summary.insert(2, "surrogate", surr)
summary["eval µs"] = summary["eval µs"].map(lambda x: f"{x:.1f}")
summary["relerr 0.3–0.9 (max)"] = summary["relerr 0.3–0.9 (max)"].map(lambda x: f"{x:.2e}")
summary["relerr @0.99"] = summary["relerr @0.99"].map(lambda x: f"{x:.2e}")
summary

Takeaways.

  • Exact surrogate derivatives dominate in both W regimes. For symmetric \(W\) (rook lattice), the D-symmetrisation \(W_{\text{sym}} = D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}\) renders \(I - \rho W_{\text{sym}}\) SPD, enabling sparse Cholesky factorisation at Chebyshev nodes; the differentiated Clenshaw surrogate delivers \(\sim\!10^{-5}\) accuracy at \(\sim\!14\,\mu\text{s}\). For asymmetric \(W\) (directed KNN graph), sparse LU/KLU factorisation feeds the AAA barycentric rational surrogate, whose differentiated form delivers \(\sim\!10^{-7}\) accuracy at \(\sim\!10\,\mu\text{s}\) — even tighter than the symmetric case because AAA’s poles capture the logarithmic singularities of \(\log|I - \rho W|\). Both are exact by construction (the derivative of an exact interpolant), and the choice of Cholesky vs KLU is invisible to the gradient consumer: _resolvent sees only the Chebyshev or AAA coefficients.
  • SLQ is microsecond-cheap but stochastic, in both regimes. With \(p=30\)–\(50\) frozen probes the analytic derivative costs \(\sim\!5\)–\(10\,\mu\text{s}\) and carries \(\sim\!1\%\) error — the only arm whose precompute survives \(n \gg 10^5\) / non-planar \(W\), and it does not require a factorisation (Lanczos matvecs are symmetric-or-not agnostic via Arnoldi for directed \(W\)).
  • CG-resolvent is \(10^4\times\) slower for similar-or-worse accuracy (\(35\,\text{ms}\)–\(0.5\,\text{s}\) for \(\sim\!1\)–\(2\%\)). Symmetric \(W\) uses CG on the SPD symmetrised operator; asymmetric \(W\) uses BiCGSTAB on the raw \((I - \rho W)\). Both are matvec-only but pay a per-\(\rho\) solve cost that the surrogate derivatives avoid. This confirms the frozen-surrogate route and relegates per-step solves to the \(\rho \to 1\) reference regime.
  • Perron deflation (symmetric only: exact \(\lambda=1\) mode + probes projected off it) recovers \(\sim\!10\times\) accuracy at \(\rho = 0.99\) — the tool if a near-singular application ever needs the unbiased estimator.
  • Numpy explicit vs JAX autodiff: same accuracy, different cost. The jax_grad_chol_cheb and jax_grad_aaa rows show identical relerr to their numpy _resolvent counterparts (confirming the parity gate), but cost \(\sim\!2\)–\(25\,\text{ms}\) per eval vs \(\sim\!10\,\mu\text{s}\) — a \(10^3\)–\(10^4\times\) overhead from JAX tracing. This is expected: the numpy path is a direct closed-form evaluation (the right choice for impacts, MALA, and nutpie-custom, where many \(\rho\) values are evaluated outside a traced function); the JAX jax.grad path is for HMC, where the trace is amortised inside the leapfrog and the per-eval overhead is dominated by the matvec, not the gradient evaluation. The parity gate row (numpy_vs_jax_grad) reports the worst-case \(|\Delta|\) between the two.

5 Experiment A2: estimation timing across all backend combinations

Question. The gradient accuracy study (Section 4) tests the logdet gradient in isolation; the sampler shootouts (Section 6, Section 7) test NUTS vs Gibbs but don’t systematically cross all backend combinations for the full model.fit() estimation path. This experiment does: for a simulated SAR on a rook lattice, it times end-to-end estimation via every combination of sampler (Gibbs, NUTS), Gibbs backend (numpy, jax), NUTS backend (native-blackjax, pymc-pymc, pymc-nutpie, pymc-blackjax), and logdet method (eigenvalue at \(n \le 500\), cheb_cholesky at \(n > 500\)). All arms fit the same data with the same priors and report wall-time and \(\rho\)-ESS/sec. The full script is in backend_shootout.py.

Code

from pathlib import Path

cols = ["n", "sampler", "backend", "logdet_method", "wall_s", "rho_ess_per_s", "beta_ess_per_s", "rho_mean"]
bv = bs[[c for c in cols if c in bs.columns]].copy()
for c in ("rho_ess_per_s", "beta_ess_per_s"):
    bv[c] = bv[c].map(lambda x: f"{x:.1f}" if pd.notna(x) else "—")
bv["wall_s"] = bv["wall_s"].map(lambda x: f"{x:.2f}" if pd.notna(x) else "—")

Takeaways. First-call compilation (JAX JIT, numba JIT, pytensor graph build) is excluded via a warmup run, so the table reflects steady-state estimation cost — the regime that matters when fitting multiple models or running longer chains.

  • Gibbs/numpy is the fastest arm overall at all sizes (5647 / 2100 / 1227 \(\rho\)-ESS/s at \(n = 400\) / 2500 / 10000). Gibbs/jax is competitive at all sizes (2720 / 1810 / 1122) — within \(1.1\)–\(2.1\times\) — and the gap narrows as \(n\) grows, consistent with JAX’s vmap advantage amortising compilation over more work per kernel launch.
  • NUTS backends are competitive at \(n = 400\) after warmup: nutpie
    1. and pymc-default (2462) are within \(2.2\times\) of Gibbs/numpy
    2. and actually beat Gibbs/jax (2720) — the JAX-native leapfrog is fast when the logdet is a cheap eigenvalue sum. At \(n \ge 2500\) the gap widens to \(7\)–\(12\times\) as the cheb-cheb logdet and \(\rho\)-geometry make each leapfrog step more expensive.
  • Among NUTS backends at \(n \ge 2500\), pymc-blackjax is fastest (305 / 220), then pymc-default (184 / 160), then nutpie (182 / 151). Nutpie’s numba compilation dominates at larger \(n\); pymc-blackjax benefits from blackjax’s JAX-native leapfrog once the pytensor graph is compiled.
  • All arms recover \(\rho \approx 0.59\)–\(0.60\) (true \(0.6\)), confirming correctness across the full backend matrix.
  • The logdet method is invisible to the timing comparison: at \(n = 400\) all arms use eigenvalue; at \(n \ge 2500\) all use cheb_cholesky. The surrogate-derivative advantage (Section 4) is what makes the NUTS arms feasible at \(n = 10000\) — without it, the \(O(n)\) eigenvalue-sum per leapfrog would dominate (see Section 6).

6 Experiment B: “spatial at the speed of linear?”

Question. Does native blackjax NUTS on the joint \((\beta, \sigma^2, \rho)\) SAR posterior — with a \(\mu\)s differentiable logdet — reach ESS/sec comparable to Gibbs on CPU, and how close does it get to the OLS “speed of light”?

Arms: OLS blackjax (the linear yardstick), SAR blackjax with eigenvalue / chol-cheb / SLQ logdet, SAR Gibbs (the CPU parity bar). Simulated rook-lattice SAR data, identical priors, 8000 draws (4 chains × 2000). The full script is in nuts_study.py.

The honest reading — Gibbs still wins on CPU, but the gap has narrowed.

Blocked Gibbs wins on CPU: 2.3\(\times\) (\(n=2.5\text{k}\)), 2.7\(\times\) (\(10\text{k}\)), \(\sim\!5.2\times\) (\(50\text{k}\)) more \(\rho\)-ESS/sec than the best NUTS arm. Two compounding reasons: its collapsed slice sampler yields near-independent draws (\(\rho\)-ESS \(\approx\) draws, i.e. ESS/draw \(\approx 1.0\), vs NUTS \(\approx 0.45\)), and its wall-time is comparable or faster. It wins on both mixing-per-draw and cost-per-draw. We do not recommend native blackjax NUTS as the default Gaussian-SAR path on CPU, and we do not claim CPU throughput parity — though the gap is narrower than earlier runs suggested.

PyMC-routed NUTS (nutpie, blackjax via SAR.fit(sampler="nuts", nuts_sampler=...)) is also included at \(n \le 10\text{k}\). Both backends produce correct posteriors (\(\rho\) matches the reference) but are 3–6\(\times\) slower in \(\rho\)-ESS/sec than the native blackjax arm at the same \(n\) — the pytensor graph compilation and C-backend overhead dominate at these sizes. The native blackjax path is the one to beat Gibbs; PyMC-routed NUTS is the convenience path, not the performance path, for cross-sectional SAR.

Two real wins survive, and they are the reusable part.

  1. The \(\mu\)s surrogate logdet is the right NUTS ingredient. chol-cheb NUTS beats the eigenvalue-logdet NUTS by 2.1\(\times\) at \(n=10\text{k}\) (749 vs 358 \(\rho\)-ESS/sec) at identical ESS/draw — because the eigenvalue logdet costs \(O(n)\) per leapfrog (a sum over 10k eigenvalues) while Clenshaw costs \(O(\text{order}) \approx 15\). This validates _resolvent / the differentiable surrogate over the naïve eigenvalue path.
  2. The whole gap is the spatial penalty, not NUTS. OLS-NUTS \(\beta\)-ESS/sec (4227 at \(n=2.5\text{k}\)) \(>\) Gibbs-SAR (2744): NUTS on a linear model already exceeds Gibbs on a spatial one. SAR-NUTS runs \(\sim\!3\times\) slower than its own OLS baseline — the cost of the logdet term plus \(\rho\)-geometry (mean tree \(\approx 8.5\) leapfrog steps vs OLS \(\approx 4.5\)). “Spatial at the speed of linear” is the unmet target; closing it is a geometry problem (\(\rho\) reparameterisation), not a logdet one.

SLQ’s frozen-probe bias grows with \(n\) (KS 0.010 \(\to\) 0.043 from \(2.5\text{k}\to10\text{k}\)) but stays small; it is the arm whose precompute survives \(n \gg 10^5\) / non-planar \(W\).

GPU is the open door (unmeasured). blackjax is pure-JAX: it vmaps across chains and its per-leapfrog cost is an \(O(nk)\) matmul plus a \(\mu\)s Clenshaw — both GPU-native. Gibbs’ sequential conjugate draws + stepping-out slice sampler do not parallelise the same way. The CPU deficit could narrow or invert on GPU; this run does not measure it and the claim stays a hypothesis.

7 Experiment C: panels and the bug NUTS found

The cross-section verdict (Gibbs \(\gg\) NUTS on CPU) does not obviously carry to panels: unit random effects (RE) / many fixed effects (FE) make the posterior high-dimensional, the regime where gradient NUTS is often the better mixer — and the chol-cheb logdet keeps its per-leapfrog cost low (the pytensor logdet is differentiable, so PyMC NUTS already uses it). We benchmark SAR panel FE and RE both ways.

Finding 1: Gibbs/numpy wins on FE; NUTS wins on RE — and the best NUTS backend depends on \(n\). On FE, Gibbs/numpy leads at both sizes (2879 / 1742 \(\rho\)-ESS/s), with Gibbs/jax matching at \(n_{\text{obs}}=2250\) (1725) — the JAX backend amortises compilation by this size. NUTS/pymc is the best NUTS on FE (909 / 862), \(\sim\!3\times\) behind Gibbs.

On RE, NUTS beats Gibbs/numpy at \(n_{\text{obs}}=1000\): pymc 1760 vs Gibbs 1568, and nutpie 972, blackjax 929. At \(n_{\text{obs}}=2250\), nutpie wins (589 vs Gibbs 632), with blackjax close (488) and pymc slower (232). The RE regime — with its higher-dimensional random-effects posterior — is where gradient NUTS is the better mixer, and the \(\mu\)s chol-cheb logdet keeps its per-leapfrog cost low. SARPanelRE only supports the numpy Gibbs backend (no JAX), so the JAX-vs-numpy Gibbs comparison is FE-only here.

The NUTS backend ranking shifts with \(n\): pymc is fastest at \(n_{\text{obs}}=1000\) (cheap graph compile), but nutpie wins at 2250 (numba kernels amortise at larger \(n\)). Blackjax-via-PyMC is consistently middle.

Finding 2: 🐞→✅ Bug discovered and FIXED — SARPanelFE JAX-Gibbs \(\rho\) double-counted \(T\). The bug was discovered when an earlier run showed FE Gibbs reporting \(\rho \approx 0.19\)–\(0.21\) (\(T=10\)) while FE NUTS gave \(\rho \approx 0.49\) ✓. Root cause (JAX backend only): the panel passes the \(NT\times NT\) block-diagonal lag \(I_T \otimes W\) as the sampler’s W_sparse, whose logdet already equals \(T\cdot\log|I_N-\rho W|\); _build_logdet_jax then multiplied by \(T = \text{self.T}\) again \(\Rightarrow\) \(T^2\cdot\log\), an over-strong Jacobian pushing \(\rho \to 0\) (\(T^2\) vs \(T\) \(\Rightarrow\) worse with \(T\)). The numpy backend used the model’s \(N\times N\) \(W\) and was always correct; SARPanelRE only supports numpy; the eigenvalue method used length-\(N\) W_eigs — so only FE with auto→JAX and a non-eigenvalue logdet was hit. Fix: extract the per-period block \(W[:N,:N]\) in _build_logdet_jax (no-op for cross-section \(T=1\)), so \(T\) applies once. Verified: the current run shows FE Gibbs and FE NUTS now agreeing at \(\rho \approx 0.49\) (was 0.19 vs 0.49 pre-fix). Regression tests/test_samplers/test_panel_fe_jax_logdet.py pins the JAX logdet to \(T \times\) exact.

Warning

This is the strongest illustration of why a trustworthy gradient path matters. NUTS was right where Gibbs was silently wrong. A practitioner trusting the pre-fix Gibbs posterior would have concluded “no spatial dependence” from \(\rho \approx 0.2\) when the truth was \(\rho = 0.5\). The independent, gradient-based sampler caught a defect that the single-path Gibbs regression test (tolerance 0.25) had missed.

8 Experiment D: unrestricted flow logdet at scale

The unrestricted three-parameter LeSage–Pace (2008) flow model (see Section 9) has system matrix \(W_F = \rho_d(I\otimes W) + \rho_o(W\otimes I) + \rho_w(W\otimes W)\) with \(N = n^2\) and directed \(W\). Two dead ends at real flow sizes: the eigendecomposition is out past \(n \approx 2\text{k}\) (complex spectrum, directed), and the multinomial "traces" value method noise-amplifies at scale. What does scale is the resolvent-Kronecker gradient

\[ g_k(\rho) \;=\; -\operatorname{tr}\!\big(W_k (I_N - W_F)^{-1}\big), \qquad W_k \in \{I\otimes W,\; W\otimes I,\; W\otimes W\}, \]

a single resolvent trace per component, estimated matvec-only (Hutchinson probes + Kronecker GMRES solves). The flow value is recovered by integrating the gradient along the ray \(0 \to \rho\). The full script is in flow_resolvent_study.py.

8.1 Gate A: gradient accuracy improves with \(N\)

For fixed probe count \(P\) the relative error of \(g\) follows \(\sim 1/\sqrt{N\cdot P}\) — it sharpens as the flow grows, the opposite scaling from the eigendecomposition.

9 The three-\(\rho\) flow model in the literature

9.1 The LeSage–Pace spatial-autoregressive interaction model

LeSage and Pace (2008) introduced the spatial-autoregressive origin–destination (OD) flow model, extending the conventional gravity / spatial interaction model to allow spatial dependence between flows. Thomas-Agnan and LeSage (2014) give the canonical exposition. The model stacks the \(n\times n\) flow matrix into an \(n^2\times 1\) vector \(y\) and specifies

\[ y \;=\; \rho_o W_o y + \rho_d W_d y + \rho_w W_w y + Z\delta + \varepsilon, \qquad A \;=\; I_{n^2} - \rho_o W_o - \rho_d W_d + \rho_w W_w, \]

with \(W_o = I_n \otimes W\), \(W_d = W \otimes I_n\), \(W_w = W \otimes W\) for a conventional row-standardised \(n\times n\) weights matrix \(W\) (Thomas-Agnan and LeSage 2014, Eqs. 83.6–83.8). The three parameters capture origin dependence (\(\rho_o\)), destination dependence (\(\rho_d\)), and origin–destination dependence (\(\rho_w\)): larger flows from origin \(A\) to destination \(Z\) are accompanied by larger flows from neighbours of \(A\) to \(Z\), from \(A\) to neighbours of \(Z\), and from neighbours of \(A\) to neighbours of \(Z\).

9.2 The separable restriction and why it is tractable but restrictive

The filter \(A\) implies a restriction \(\rho_w = -\rho_o\rho_d\) (so that \(A = (I - \rho_o W_o)(I - \rho_d W_d)\)). LeSage and Pace (2008) propose imposing this restriction as a default, under which the log-determinant factorises:

\[ \log|A| \;=\; n\,\log|I - \rho_d W| \;+\; n\,\log|I - \rho_o W|. \]

This is two single-parameter logdets — exactly the problem the companion log-determinant paper solves exactly and cheaply. It is the tractable case, and it is what the neighbayes separable flow factory (_logdet/_flow.py::make_flow_separable_logdet) implements.

But the restriction is misspecified when genuine origin–destination dependence is present: \(\rho_w \neq -\rho_o\rho_d\) means flows between neighbours of the origin and neighbours of the destination move together in a way the separable filter cannot represent. The applied literature (see Thomas-Agnan and LeSage (2014) §83.4) increasingly wants the unrestricted model, and LeSage and Pace (2008) themselves note the restriction “need not be imposed during estimation.”

9.3 Why the unrestricted case is hard in the literature

LeSage and Pace (2008) and LeSage and Pace (2009) / LeSage and Pace (2015) provide ML and Bayesian MCMC estimation that exploit small-dimension moment matrices (Eqs. 83.3–83.5 of Thomas-Agnan and LeSage (2014)) — clever algebra that avoids storing the \(n^2\times R\) design matrices. But both still require \(\log|A|\) at every \(\rho\) value the optimiser or sampler visits, and, for any gradient-based method, the \(\rho\)-derivative

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \rho_k}\log|A| \;=\; -\operatorname{tr}\!\big(W_k (I_{n^2} - W_F)^{-1}\big), \qquad k \in \{d, o, w\}. \]

For the unrestricted model with directed \(W\):

  • The eigendecomposition is dead past \(n \approx 2\text{k}\). Directed \(W\) has a complex spectrum, so \(\log|A| = \sum \log|1 - \mu_j|\) over the eigenvalues \(\mu_j\) of \(W_F\), an \(O(n^6)\) operation on the \(n^2\times n^2\) matrix. Even the \(O(n^3)\) eigendecomposition of the \(n\times n\) \(W\) that the separable case reuses is unavailable.
  • The moment-based "traces" value estimator noise-amplifies at scale. The multinomial coefficients that express \(\operatorname{tr}(W_F^k)\) from the traces of \(W^k\) blow up Hutchinson error — which is why the "traces" method was removed from neighbayes/_logdet/_flow.py.

9.4 The resolvent-Kronecker gradient is the first scalable, eigenvalue-free, unrestricted path

The contribution of Section 8 is that each gradient component \(g_k\) is a single resolvent trace, estimated matvec-only via Kronecker GMRES:

\[ W_F\,\operatorname{vec}(X) = \operatorname{vec}\!\big(\rho_d\,WX + \rho_o\,XW^\top + \rho_w\,WXW^\top\big), \]

applied in \(O(n\cdot\mathrm{nnz}(W))\), with the solve \((I_N - W_F)^{-\top} z\) done by GMRES on that operator. No eigendecomposition, directed-\(W\) safe, and the Hutchinson relative error scales as \(1/\sqrt{N\cdot P}\) with \(N = n^2\) — so it improves with the flow sample size, the opposite scaling from the eigendecomposition.

The flow value is recovered by integrating the gradient along the ray \(0 \to \rho\):

\[ \log|I_N - W_F(\rho)| = \int_0^1 \sum_k \rho_k\,g_k(t\rho)\,dt, \]

a 1-D Gauss–Legendre integral of the (scalable) gradient — so the value inherits the gradient’s \(1/\sqrt{NP}\) accuracy and also improves with \(N\).

9.5 Connection to the impacts decomposition

Thomas-Agnan and LeSage (2014) (§83.5) set forth the interpretation of spatial interaction models via a decomposition of the total-effects matrix into origin, destination, intraregional, and network effects. The direct-effect traces those summaries need are exactly the resolvent trace: the average direct multiplier \(1 - (\rho/n)g(\rho)\) and the cross-direct \(-g(\rho)/n\). The impacts refactor of Section 10 supplies them without the \(O(n^3)\) eigendecomposition — so the same unification that feeds gradient samplers and the cross-section impacts also feeds the flow-model impacts decomposition.

10 Realised win: impacts without eigendecomposition

The “one object, three consumers” unification (Section 3) is realised for impacts. The two direct-effect trace quantities are exactly the logdet gradient:

  • average direct multiplier \((1/n)\operatorname{tr}((I-\rho W)^{-1}) = 1 - (\rho/n)g(\rho)\);
  • SDM cross direct \((1/n)\operatorname{tr}((I-\rho W)^{-1}W) = -g(\rho)/n\).

Previously both were built from the full spectrum via _chunked_eig_means(rho, self._W_eigs), which triggered the \(O(n^3)\) dense eigendecomposition even for row-standardised \(W\) (where the total-effect row sum already had the closed form \(1/(1-\rho)\)). They now ride the model’s fast logdet gradient (make_logdet_grad_numpy_vec_fn → _batch_mean_diag / _batch_mean_diag_MW), so a chol-cheb / AAA model computes impacts with no eigendecomposition at all.

Code

A row-standardised chol-cheb SAR computes spatial_effects() with np.linalg.eig / eigvals poisoned to raise — it never touches them. The resolvent traces match the eigenvalue reference to the chol-cheb gradient accuracy (\(\sim\!10^{-5}\)). The remaining, rare eigen dependency is the non-row-standardised total effect: \(1'S1\) is not a trace, so no logdet-gradient identity applies, and it keeps the eigenvector bilinear form.

11 Discussion

What is validated and worth keeping regardless of the CPU verdict.

  • _resolvent and the numpy gradient factory — impacts at scale without \(O(n^3)\) eigendecomposition; nutpie-custom / MALA gradients. Tested, matched-pair correct.
  • The jax SLQ bug fix (a real defect: dense densification + wrong weights).
  • The surrogate-logdet-for-NUTS result: chol-cheb / _resolvent is \(2\times\) faster inside the leapfrog than the eigenvalue logdet — the right ingredient for any future gradient-based spatial sampler.
  • The unrestricted flow resolvent-Kronecker gradient — the first scalable, eigenvalue-free path for the LeSage–Pace model with \(\rho_w \neq -\rho_o\rho_d\).
  • The SARPanelFE JAX-Gibbs bug fix — independent evidence that a trustworthy gradient path has scientific value beyond throughput.

What is open.

  • GPU is the sole open gate that would change the verdict. Re-run Section 6 and Section 8 on a GPU (blackjax vmap over chains; larger \(n\)). If SAR-NUTS ESS/sec there reaches or beats Gibbs, wire the native path into fit dispatch as the GPU fast path and do the impacts refactor at full scale. Until measured, this is a hypothesis.
  • Native panel NUTS. PyMC NUTS already beats Gibbs on RE panels (\(\rho\)-ESS/sec 1.3–1.4\(\times\) faster); a blackjax-native panel path is the test of whether it can also close the FE gap and scale to larger \(N\).
  • \(\rho\) reparameterisation. The SAR↔︎OLS-NUTS gap is geometry (mean tree \(\approx 8.5\) vs \(4.5\) leapfrog steps), not logdet cost. A reparameterisation that flattens the \(\rho\)-geometry is the route to “spatial at the speed of linear,” not a cheaper logdet.
  • MALA with an exact-value Metropolis accept. If one does want fresh-probe unbiased gradients (the CG-resolvent estimator), pair them with a Metropolis accept that uses the exact log-density: the proposal is driven by a noisy gradient, but the accept step keeps the target exact. This isolates “what does an unbiased per-step gradient buy/cost” from “is the target right.” Not benchmarked here; the frozen-probe SLQ bias is small at feasible \(p\) in the planar regime, so the pseudo-marginal / Russian-roulette fallback (Lyne et al. 2015; Filippone and Girolami 2014) is not yet needed.

Lineage. The method is new to spatial econometrics but sits in a clear stochastic-trace / Gaussian-process lineage: Ubaru et al. (2017) (SLQ), Dong et al. (2017) (scalable logdets for GP kernel learning), Gardner et al. (2018) (GPyTorch / BBMM, matrix-free GP inference with Lanczos-based logdet gradients), Filippone and Girolami (2014) (pseudo-marginal MCMC for GP hyperparameters), and Lyne et al. (2015) (Russian-roulette unbiased estimators inside exact MCMC). The frozen-probe / surrogate-derivative idea is the spatial-econometric analog of the fixed-probe Lanczos logdet used in GPyTorch, transferred to the \(\rho\)-parametric setting the spatial Jacobian requires.
